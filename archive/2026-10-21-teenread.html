Tonight's Shooting Stars Are Crumbs From Halley's Comet

The Orionid meteor shower peaks right now, on 21 October. Every streak you catch is a speck of dust shed by one of the most famous comets ever, burning up high above your head.

Read more

What happened

Every October, Earth ploughs through a stream of dust left behind by Halley's Comet. Sky at Night Magazine reports that the 2026 Orionid meteor shower reaches its peak on 21 October. That makes tonight, and last night, the best chances to see it. A meteor is what most people call a "shooting star", but it is not a star at all. It is a tiny grain of comet dust hitting our atmosphere so fast that it heats up and glows for a split second.

There is one catch this year. The magazine says the Moon will be nearly full during the peak, so its glow will wash out the fainter meteors. You will only see the brighter ones, but those are the most dramatic anyway.

The numbers

  • Peak time: about 12:00 UT on 21 October 2026, so the nights of 20/21 and 21/22 October are the best.
  • The shower runs from 2 October to 7 November, so you can still spot stray Orionids for a couple of weeks.
  • Orionids hit the air at about 66 kilometres per second. That is fast enough to cross London in under a second.
  • Most of the dust grains are smaller than a grain of sand.
  • Halley's Comet last came near the Sun in 1986 and will be back in 2061.

The science behind it

As a comet travels near the Sun, it warms up and sheds dust, a bit like a muddy dog shaking itself off. Over thousands of years that dust spreads out along the comet's path. Earth crosses Halley's trail twice a year: in May, when we get the Eta Aquariid shower, and in October, when we get the Orionids. The meteors seem to fly out of one spot near the constellation Orion, called the radiant. That is a perspective trick, just as snowflakes seem to rush at you from one point when you drive through snow.

How to watch

You don't need a telescope. Find somewhere as dark as you can, keep the Moon behind you, lie back and give your eyes about 20 minutes to adjust. Put your phone away, because its bright screen undoes that adjustment. Orion climbs higher after midnight, so the hours before dawn are usually best.

Why it's cool: Halley's Comet won't be back until 2061, but tonight you can watch pieces of it light up the sky. The dust may have broken off the comet centuries ago.

Think about it: By 2061 you will be in your late forties. Where do you imagine you'll be standing when Halley's Comet itself comes back, and who would you want with you?

Reading the Universe's First Five Minutes in 15 Tiny Galaxies

Astronomers have made one of the most precise measurements yet of how much helium was made just after the Big Bang. Their trick was to study small, "untouched" galaxies that work like cosmic time capsules.

Read more

What happened

An international team used one of the world's most powerful telescopes to study 15 small, distant galaxies that have barely changed since the early Universe. From them, the scientists measured how much helium formed in the first five minutes after the Big Bang. ScienceDaily describes the result as more precise than any measurement before it.

The numbers

  • 15 "pristine" galaxies were studied, meaning galaxies whose gas has been changed very little by stars.
  • The clues inside them date back to nearly 13.8 billion years ago.
  • The helium they measured was made in roughly the first 5 minutes of cosmic history.
  • The Big Bang theory rests on 3 main lines of evidence: the Universe is still expanding, there is leftover heat called the cosmic microwave background, and there are set amounts of light elements like helium.

The science behind it

Right after the Big Bang, the whole Universe was a fantastically hot soup of particles. For a few minutes it was hot and dense enough for those particles to fuse into the lightest elements: mostly hydrogen, plus helium and tiny traces of others. Then it cooled and the fusing stopped. How much helium got made depends on the exact conditions in those minutes, so measuring it is like reading a recipe card from the start of time.

The problem is that stars also make helium, and they have been doing it for billions of years. The usual approach is to measure lots of galaxies and then work backwards to guess the starting amount. This team did something different. They picked galaxies so pristine that their stars have hardly mixed anything new into the gas, which gives a cleaner look at the original ingredients.

The more exact the helium number is, the better scientists can test whether the laws of physics behaved as expected when the Universe was only minutes old.

Why it's cool: Some of the helium in a party balloon was made in those first few minutes of the Universe, so you've held a piece of the Big Bang.

Think about it: If the helium amount had come out very different from what theory predicts, what do you think scientists should do: change the theory, or check the measurement again first?

ScienceDaily / NASA · Oct 20, 2026

16 Galaxies, Four Telescopes, One Big Cosmic Drama

NASA has released a new gallery of 16 galaxies by combining images from Chandra, Webb, Hubble and other telescopes. Together they show black hole jets, bursts of star birth and galaxies smashing together.

Read more

What happened

NASA has put together a collection of 16 galaxies, each seen through several telescopes at once. The pictures show huge jets blasting out from black holes, regions where stars are forming at an explosive rate, and galaxies crashing into each other. According to the release, galaxies are always being shaped by gravity, exploding stars, black holes and run-ins with their neighbours.

The numbers

  • 16 galaxies are in the new gallery.
  • It mixes images from NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, the James Webb Space Telescope, Hubble and other observatories.
  • Three kinds of drama are on show: black hole jets, explosive star formation and cosmic collisions.
  • Chandra picks up X-rays from gas heated to millions of degrees, far hotter than the surface of the Sun.

How do they know?

Every telescope sees a different kind of light. Hubble mostly sees visible light, the kind our eyes see. Webb sees infrared, which slips through dust clouds and shows young stars hiding inside. Chandra sees X-rays, which come from the most violent places, like super-hot gas swirling around black holes. Our eyes can't see X-rays or infrared, so scientists give each kind of light a colour and then stack the layers. It works a bit like a doctor combining an X-ray with a regular photo to get the full picture.

Seeing the same galaxy in several kinds of light lets astronomers connect causes and effects. For example, they can spot whether a black hole's jet seems to be setting off star birth nearby, or blowing the gas away so no new stars can form.

Why it's cool: NASA says these images give clues about our own Milky Way's past and future. That matters because astronomers think our galaxy will have its own close encounter with the Andromeda galaxy billions of years from now.

Think about it: If you could add a fifth telescope that sees a brand-new kind of signal, what would you most want it to reveal about galaxies?

A Spiral Galaxy, Seen From the Side

Hubble has taken a new image of galaxy NGC 1827 edge-on, so it looks like a glittering streak striped with dark ribbons of dust. It's a bit like seeing a vinyl record from the side.

Read more

What happened

The Hubble Space Telescope has photographed the spiral galaxy NGC 1827 using its Advanced Camera for Surveys. Seen from above, a spiral galaxy looks like a pinwheel. We happen to see NGC 1827 almost exactly side-on, so its spiral arms are hidden. What shows instead is a thin, sparkling edge full of stars, with brown bands of dust woven through it.

The numbers

  • NGC 1827 is about 37 million light-years from Earth.
  • That means the light in this photo left the galaxy about 37 million years ago, roughly 29 million years after the dinosaurs died out.
  • The image was taken with Hubble's Advanced Camera for Surveys (ACS).
  • A light-year is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.5 trillion kilometres.

The science behind it

Spiral galaxies are surprisingly flat. Most of their stars, gas and dust sit in a thin disc, a bit like icing on a very wide, very thin pancake. When we see the disc edge-on, the dust lines up between us and the stars behind it. The dust soaks up and dims their light, which is why it shows up as dark brown ribbons.

Edge-on galaxies help astronomers measure how thick a galaxy's disc is, how its dust is spread out, and whether stars puff up above and below the main disc. Those details are hard to see when you look at a spiral face-on.

Look up

You have actually seen an edge-on spiral yourself. We live inside the disc of the Milky Way, which is also a spiral galaxy. On a very dark night the faint band called the Milky Way, with its dark dusty lanes, is our own galaxy seen from the inside, edge-on.

Why it's cool: A photo like this gives you an outsider's view of something you can only ever see from inside: the shape of our own galaxy.

Think about it: If astronomers in NGC 1827 were looking back at us, would they see the Milky Way face-on, edge-on or tilted? How could you work that out?

Scientists Make Old Cells Molecularly "Young" Again in the Lab

Researchers turned blood cells from an 80-year-old into brain stem cells that test as younger than 20. That is more than 60 years of cell ageing undone in a dish. It is lab research, not a treatment.

Read more

What happened

Scientists at the University of Bonn in Germany took blood cells from an 80-year-old person and changed them into neural stem cells. Neural stem cells are the starter cells that can grow into different kinds of brain cells. When the team measured the "molecular age" of the new cells, it came out at less than 20. In other words, the cells looked more than 60 years younger at the molecular level.

To be clear: this happened in laboratory dishes. Nobody was made younger. The researchers see it as a new way to study how cells grow younger and what goes wrong in diseases linked to ageing, such as Alzheimer's.

The numbers

  • Donor age: 80 years old.
  • Molecular age of the new stem cells: under 20.
  • Ageing reversed at the cell level: more than 60 years.
  • Starting cells: blood cells. Ending cells: neural (brain) stem cells.

The science behind it

Your birthday age and your cells' "molecular age" are not quite the same thing. As we get older, tiny chemical tags build up on our DNA in patterns that change over the years, a bit like rings in a tree trunk. Scientists can read those patterns to estimate how old a cell seems. Researchers have also known for years that cells can be "reprogrammed" back to a flexible, stem-cell-like state. Here the Bonn team showed that going from blood cell to brain stem cell also cleared away decades of those age marks.

Why bother with brain cells? You can't easily take living brain cells from a person to study. Growing them from a simple blood sample gives scientists a way to compare young-looking and old-looking cells side by side.

Why it's cool: A single blood sample could become a tiny laboratory for studying the brain. That kind of tool could help future scientists, maybe including you, work out how diseases like Alzheimer's begin.

China's First Freshwater Cave Snail Has No Eyes, and Only Three Have Been Found

Deep in a high mountain cave in China, scientists have found a ghostly, eyeless snail that spends its whole life in darkness. It is a brand-new species, and the first freshwater cave snail ever recorded in China.

Read more

What happened

During fieldwork in August 2025, researchers exploring a high-altitude mountain cave in China came across tiny snails living in the cave's water. The snails had no eyes. The team realised they were a species no one had described before and named it Erhaia dijiang. It is the first freshwater snail adapted to cave life ever recorded in China, which opens a new chapter in the study of the country's underground wildlife.

The numbers

  • Only 3 living snails of the new species were found.
  • Fieldwork date: August 2025. The discovery was reported this week.
  • Eyes: 0. The snail is completely blind.
  • Firsts: the first freshwater cave snail on record in China.

The science behind it

Animals that live their whole lives in caves often end up looking alike, even when they aren't related. Many lose their eyes, because in total darkness eyes are useless and cost energy to grow and maintain. Many also lose their colour and turn pale or see-through, because there is no sunlight to protect against and no one to hide from by sight. Biologists call this convergent evolution: different animals arriving at the same "design" because they face the same challenge.

Cave water is also short on food, so cave animals tend to be small and slow, and to live a long time. Instead of seeing, they find their way by touch and chemical "smells" in the water.

Why so few?

Finding only three living snails suggests this species may be very rare, or may live deep in cracks where people can't reach. Either way, it shows how much life is still hidden underground, even today.

Why it's cool: In 2026 there are still animals no one has ever seen, sitting in the dark beneath our feet and waiting to be found.

Think about it: With only three snails known, should scientists collect more to study them, or leave the cave alone to protect them? How would you decide?

A Rock Went 90 Kilometres Down Into Earth and Came Back With a Surprise

Scientists studying a rock from Papua New Guinea found it had been dragged more than 90 km under the surface. On the way it went from unusually hot to fairly cold, which doesn't fit the usual theories.

Read more

What happened

Geologists from the University of Göttingen in Germany studied a rock found in Papua New Guinea. They worked out that it had sunk more than 90 kilometres below Earth's surface before somehow coming back up. The rock also kept a record of the temperatures it went through, and that record was strange. It was unusually hot at first, then switched to relatively cold conditions. This pattern challenges established ideas about what happens deep inside Earth, and could change how scientists understand moving tectonic plates, earthquakes and volcanoes.

The numbers

  • Depth reached: more than 90 kilometres.
  • That is about 10 Mount Everests stacked up, and roughly 8 times deeper than the deepest part of the ocean.
  • Location: Papua New Guinea, a country in the southwest Pacific Ocean.
  • The research was first reported earlier this month. ScienceDaily ran it on 21 October.

How do they know?

Earth's outer shell is broken into giant pieces called tectonic plates. In some places one plate slides underneath another and sinks into the hot interior, a process called subduction. Rocks dragged down this way get squeezed and heated, and their minerals slowly change into new crystals. Each mix of crystals only forms at certain pressures and temperatures. By reading those crystals, geologists can tell how deep a rock went and how hot it got, a bit like reading the stamps in a passport.

Usually, a rock sinking deeper is expected to warm up steadily. This one recorded heat first and colder conditions later. That hints the area may have gone through more than one kind of subduction over time.

Why it matters

Subduction zones cause many of the world's biggest earthquakes and build chains of volcanoes, like the Pacific "Ring of Fire". Knowing how heat moves down there helps scientists understand these powerful natural events.

Why it's cool: No drill has ever gone even close to 90 km deep. This rock made the trip by itself and brought back a story that no machine could.

Meet the Chunkiest "Squid" of the Jurassic

A 160-million-year-old fossil from Wyoming belongs to the fattest belemnite ever found. Belemnites were barrel-shaped relatives of squid, and fossil hunters there nickname their remains "squid butts".

Read more

What happened

About 160 million years ago, a shallow inland sea covered much of what is now the western United States. Squid-like animals called belemnites swam in it by the millions. Their fossils are so common in Wyoming's Sundance Formation that locals call them "squid butts". But one new species stands out. It is named Wyoteuthis linsterorum, and its hard inner skeleton is so thick and barrel-shaped that scientists call it the "fattest" belemnite ever identified.

Only two examples are known. One was discovered by Linster, who made sure it went to scientists for study. Wyoming paleontologist and co-author Jessica Lippincott stressed how unusual the find is, even in a place with so many belemnite fossils. The study was first announced in September; ScienceDaily featured it this week.

The numbers

  • Age: about 160 million years (the Jurassic period).
  • Typical Wyoming belemnite: 5-10 cm long and only 1-2 cm wide, shaped like a bullet.
  • The new species: about 10 cm long but a whopping 6 cm wide, the thickest of its kind.
  • Whole animal, including arms: about 60 cm, or two feet, roughly the length of a skateboard.
  • Known specimens: just 2.

How do they know?

Belemnites had soft bodies like squid, but also a solid, bullet-shaped part at the back called a rostrum. Soft tissue rots away, so the rostrum is usually all that becomes a fossil. Scientists compare its size and shape with complete belemnite fossils found elsewhere to estimate how big the whole animal was. The researchers think this chunky species may have hunted smaller squid-like animals. They also think it may have been a surprise survivor from a family that was believed to have already died out.

Why it's cool: One person's careful choice to hand over a fossil turned a "squid butt" into a brand-new species. Anyone with sharp eyes can help science.

Think about it: Why might a belemnite evolve a much heavier, thicker rostrum than its relatives? Think about swimming, balance or hunting.

The "Smoking Gun" That Proves a Lost Viking Church in Greenland

For 20 years, archaeologists suspected that ruins near Erik the Red's settlement were a Norse church. This summer they finally found the proof they needed: a careful Christian burial.

Read more

What happened

In the early 2000s, researchers in southern Greenland found a rectangular stone wall with the remains of a stone building in the middle. The layout looked like a Norse church inside its churchyard wall. To be sure, though, they needed a cemetery, because Norse churches had their graves right beside them.

In 2025 the team went back. On the very last day of digging they began finding bits of charcoal, a sign that people had been there, and then what looked like a decayed human bone. They returned this August and uncovered a whole skeleton, laid out in a way that matches a Norse Christian burial. "This is 'the smoking gun,'" archaeologist Madsen said in a statement. There is now no doubt the building was a church.

The numbers

  • The site was first spotted as a possible church in the early 2000s.
  • The first clues to a grave turned up on the final day of the 2025 dig.
  • A full skeleton was uncovered in August 2026.
  • Reported: this is the first Norse church found in Greenland in more than 60 years.
  • The Norse who lived in Greenland vanished in the 1400s, and no one knows exactly why.

Some background

Around the year 985, a Norse explorer known as Erik the Red led settlers from Iceland to Greenland. Their communities of farmers lasted for roughly 400 years. Then, in the 15th century, they disappeared. Historians have argued about why for a long time. Possible reasons include a cooling climate, trade with Europe drying up, or people simply moving away. Each newly found building, such as this church near Erik the Red's settlement, adds another clue to how these communities lived and changed. The work is linked to the National Museum of Denmark.

Why it's cool: It's real-life detective work. A hunch from two decades ago was finally proven on the last day of a dig, which is a good reason never to give up on a good question.

India's First Trip to the Moon Launched 18 Years Ago Today

On 21 October 2008, India launched Chandrayaan-1, its first deep-space mission. The spacecraft helped find ice hiding in dark craters on the Moon, and it set India on course to become a major space nation.

Read more

What happened

Chandrayaan means "moon craft" in Sanskrit. India's Chandrayaan-1 lifted off from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre on an Indian-built rocket, the PSLV-XL. It was the country's first mission beyond Earth's orbit. Because of time zones, the launch happened in the evening of 21 October in the eastern United States but early in the morning of 22 October at the launch site in India.

The spacecraft orbited the Moon carrying instruments from several countries. One of them was NASA's Mini-SAR, a radar that could "see" inside shadowy craters near the Moon's poles where sunlight never reaches. It found water ice in many of them.

The numbers

  • Launch: 8:52 p.m. EDT on 21 October 2008, which was about 6:22 a.m. on 22 October in India.
  • Rocket: the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV-XL).
  • Mini-SAR found more than 40 small craters holding water ice.
  • Those craters are 2-15 km (1-9 miles) across.
  • Fifteen years later, in 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 became the first mission to land near the Moon's south pole.

The science behind it

Near the Moon's poles, the Sun stays low on the horizon, so the floors of some deep craters stay in shadow all the time. They are among the coldest places in the Solar System, cold enough to keep ice frozen for billions of years. Radar works by sending out radio waves and listening for how they bounce back. Ice reflects them in a telltale way, so Mini-SAR could pick it out even in total darkness.

Why it matters

Water on the Moon is like treasure for future explorers. It can be drunk, and it can also be split into oxygen to breathe and hydrogen for rocket fuel. That is a big reason so many countries now want to visit the lunar south pole.

Why it's cool: One mission from 2008 changed what we know about the Moon and helped make space a real career path for young people across India and beyond.
